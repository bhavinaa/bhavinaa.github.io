Oil Crisis Gray Swan Detector

View on GitHub

The problem

A major disruption at the Strait of Hormuz — a blockade, a tanker seizure, a strike on Gulf infrastructure — would spike oil prices fast, and the options market would reprice long before headlines fully explain why. I wanted a system that watches for that regime shift continuously, rather than relying on me noticing a headline and reacting hours too late.

What I built

The detector pulls live data from 8+ sources — EIA spot and STEO data, OANDA CFD pricing for after-hours coverage, Hyperliquid perpetuals when CME is closed, geopolitical risk indices, refinery utilization, and OSINT tanker-tracking accounts on Twitter — and scores five signal categories on a 0–100 scale: crisis price signals, macro stress, supply flow, news/escalation, and market microstructure.

Those scores combine into a composite risk level (green / amber / red), with a set of hard overrides that force an instant red alert regardless of the composite score — things like the Saudi riyal peg deviating past a threshold, Cushing storage dropping below a floor, or an LLM-confirmed tier-1 keyword hit like "tanker seized" or "export ban." When a tweet from a tracked OSINT account comes in, an LLM classifies its urgency and extracts barrel figures to feed the physical supply model.

On top of the risk score, the system runs a Bayesian model estimating days-until-threshold using Brownian motion with drift, with CUSUM change-point detection to catch regime shifts early. When risk crosses into amber or red, it switches from selling put spreads to harvest premium into recommending USO call positions, complete with Black-Scholes Greeks, a repricing scenario grid, and a concrete exit ladder (sell 50% at 3x, 25% at 5x, let 25% ride, stop loss at 40% down).

What made it hard

  • Reconciling data sources with different freshness and reliability — building a spot-price hierarchy (EIA daily → OANDA after-hours → manual broker data) so the system degrades gracefully instead of trusting stale numbers.
  • Avoiding false positives from noisy OSINT signals — this is why source convergence across multiple independent accounts matters more than any single tweet.
  • Keeping the Bayesian trend model honest across changes to the scoring model itself, by regime-filtering which historical runs it's allowed to learn from.
A single alarming headline is noise. Five independent signal categories moving together is a regime shift.

This started as a way to combine an interest in geopolitics and options markets with an actual engineering problem — turning something I'd otherwise track by anxiously refreshing Twitter into a system that only interrupts me when the evidence actually adds up.
